# Write like a person, not like AI

The writer's style guide and its last step before every draft is saved. Two parts:
how a post is shaped, and **the rewrite pass**, which runs on every draft and every
revision, no exceptions.

Rank: the honesty rules in `craft.md` and `sector-rules.md` beat everything here.
Once a person has voice notes (`/people/<handle>/memory/voice.md`), their voice beats
the style rules below where the two disagree: if they use dashes or fragments, keep
them at their rate. The rewrite pass still runs; it removes the model's habits, not
theirs.

Built from three sources, adapted for LinkedIn posts and for a writer that may never
invent a fact:

## Part 1. The shape of a post

- **Line 1 is a statement, never a question.** It carries the whole surprise: who,
  what, the number. When the material has an exact number with something it
  belongs to ("$10 per million output tokens", "1,130 pull requests"), lead with it.
  No "Here's what…", no "Stop X, start Y", no "Ever wondered…".
- **One idea.** If you need "also", it is two posts.
- **Say the point where it belongs.** Do not set up a reveal ("The figure I would plan
  around is a different one:"). Write the figure.
- **One contrast and one list of three per post, at most.** And only when the
  material really has two sides or three parts.
- **Concrete over general.** Names, dates, amounts, places, the tool, the city. Take
  them only from the material; never add one to sound human.
- **Opinion in the first person, plainly.** "I'd plan around the output limit" beats
  an aphorism about what the output limit means.
- **Layout:** one or two sentences per paragraph, blank lines between. That is
  LinkedIn's normal layout, not a tell. The tell is fragments for drama inside it.
- **The close:** one specific question the reader can answer from their own work, or
  the concrete takeaway. Never "What do you think?", "Agree?", "Thoughts?", "Tag
  someone who…". A one-line P.S. is fine when there is a real follow-up.
- **Length:** 900–1,900 characters for most posts; hard cap 3,000.
- **No hashtags, no links in the body** unless the person's voice notes say they use
  them.

## Part 2. The rewrite pass (every draft, every revision)

Run it on your own draft before you save it to `drafts`, and again after any edit you
make. Treat the draft as text to edit. Do it in four steps.

1. **Mark the tells.** Read the whole draft once and mark every pattern from Part 3,
   strongest first. Look at paragraph shape as well as sentences: a contrast split
   across two sentences, three parallel examples, or the same kind of closer after
   every paragraph is the same tell at a larger scale. Score vocabulary per
   paragraph: three or more markers in one paragraph means rewrite that paragraph;
   one marker alone is usually fine.
2. **Rewrite.** Keep every supported claim and every source. You may shorten, merge or
   split paragraphs and change the order. Do not add a fact, name, number, date,
   quote or citation that is not in the material. If a sentence needs a detail you
   do not have, write a simpler sentence. A reaction or opinion is allowed when the
   person would have one; a factual claim is not.
3. **Check it.** Read it as the reader will, top to bottom. Ask "what still sounds AI
   here?" and fix it. Then look again for the tells that survive rewrites most often:
   not-X-but-Y contrasts, one-line closers, triads, dashes, and sayings that sound
   deep. Check you did not drop or change any claim or number.
4. **Guard against over-correcting.** Rewrites have their own fingerprint. Did you
   create fragment runs ("Short. Clear. Done."), "The result?" reveals, one-word
   paragraphs, or a long/short/long/short seesaw? Merge them back into sentences. Did
   you add a hedge, a "to be honest", or a confession the material never had? Remove
   it. Did you flatten the voice so nothing is left: no reaction, no concrete detail,
   every dash and every list of three gone? Restore what was there. A clean draft gets
   two or three touches, not a quota. When unsure whether something is the person or
   the model, leave it.

Then save. In your summary for the reviewer, say in one line what the pass changed
("cut two closers and a not-X-but-Y; first line now leads with the number").

## Part 3. The tells

### A. Staging instead of stating (the strongest; act on one sighting)

1. **Not X but Y.** "It's not X, it's Y", "not just X but Y", "X rather than Y", the
   split form ("This does not mean X. It means Y."), the clipped tail ("…, no
   guessing"). The negative half names something nobody claimed so the positive half
   sounds bigger. State the point. Keep a contrast only when it corrects a belief the
   reader actually holds, and then once per post.
   - Before: *That gate, more than the score, is the thing to watch.*
   - After: *You can't call it yet. It launches to an approved list first.*
2. **One-line closers and dramatic fragments.** A one-sentence paragraph that restates
   the one before it ("That distinction matters.", "That is the real win.", "Read that
   again.", "Let that sink in."); a line after an example that names what it showed
   ("The message was clear."); a run of fragments ("No setup. No waiting. Just
   results."); a word in capitals for drama. Cut a closer that repeats. Keep a short
   sentence only when it adds a new fact.
3. **Sayings that sound deep.** "The real question is", "at its core", "what really
   matters", "the heart of the matter", "X is the Y of Z", "X becomes a trap", "X is
   not a tool but a mirror", "the currency of", "the honest part", "the wall most
   products hit". An ordinary point dressed as a hidden truth. Write the specific
   claim.
   - Before: *Output length is the wall most AI products hit.*
   - After: *Most AI products I look at assume a short reply.*
4. **Staged run-up before the point.** "Here's the thing", "Here's what you need to
   know", "Let's break this down", "Honestly?", "Look,", "Real talk", "The figure I'd
   plan around is a different one:". Delete the run-up; the next sentence was the
   point.
5. **Arguing with no one.** "This isn't about X", "I'm not saying", "To be clear",
   "Don't get me wrong", "You might think… but", "A tempting approach would be". It
   answers an objection nobody raised. Remove it; if it holds a real claim, state the
   claim.
6. **Reveal bridges.** "The result?", "The catch?", "The kicker?", "Plot twist:",
   "Spoiler:", "Why? Because…". Delete the bridge and let the next sentence stand.

### B. Rhythm by rule

7. **Forced triads.** Three adjectives, three parallel examples, three short facts
   then a lesson. Use as many items as the material has. One natural list of three
   per post is fine; stacked or perfectly parallel triads are not.
8. **Repeated sentence openings.** Several sentences in a row starting with the same
   word or subject. Merge, change the subject, or start with the action.
9. **Dashes everywhere.** A dash used to avoid choosing how two clauses relate. Cap at
   about one em dash per 100 words; use a full stop, comma, colon or parentheses for
   the rest. Zero is also fine. A person whose voice notes show dashes keeps them.
10. **Fragment runs and seesaw rhythm.** At most two standalone fragments in a post.
    Never alternate long and short sentences on purpose. Edit rhythm only where a
    whole paragraph reads flat, and then change one sentence.
11. **Stacked qualifiers.** "Could potentially", "might arguably", "it's also
    possible that", "to be fair". Keep a qualifier only when the source supports the
    doubt (a preprint, a forecast, a single study).
12. **Passive voice that hides who acted.** Name the actor when the source does.

### C. Inflation and borrowed authority

13. **Words models overuse.** Score by density (three or more in a paragraph means
    rewrite): significant, crucial, notably, particularly, comprehensive, insights,
    robust, leverage, foster, landscape (abstract), nuanced, multifaceted, holistic,
    streamline, elevate, empower, enhance, pivotal, key (adjective), highlight (verb),
    underscore, showcase, delve, tapestry, testament, intricate, meticulous,
    interplay, additionally (to open a sentence), game-changer, unlock, deep dive,
    navigate (anything that is not a road).
14. **The 2026 LinkedIn layer.** Once human idiom, now model idiom: "quietly"
    (shipped, became), "X matters." as a sentence, "compounds", "the signal", "the
    work", "built different", "load-bearing", "doing the heavy lifting", "that's the
    real story". Each counts as a marker; "let that sink in" and "that's the real
    story" go on sight.
15. **Inflated significance.** "Marks a pivotal moment", "reflects a broader shift",
    "setting the stage for", "an evolving landscape", "the future of X". Keep the fact,
    drop the significance. End on the last concrete fact or the question.
16. **Vague connection.** "Linked to", "tied to", "associated with" when the source
    says exactly how. Name the relationship; keep the vague word only when the source
    is vague (an observational study really is "linked to").
17. **Shallow -ing riders.** "…, highlighting the need for", "…, underscoring",
    "…, ensuring", "…, reflecting". Keep the fact; drop the rider.
18. **Sales language.** Groundbreaking, renowned, stunning, a diverse array of,
    exemplifies, commitment to. Say what the thing is and does.
19. **Borrowed authority.** "Experts say", "many believe", "industry observers",
    "several reports". Name who said it and link it on the draft, or say it as the
    person's own view, or cut it.
20. **Avoiding "is" and "has".** "Serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "features",
    "offers" when you mean is or has.

### D. Formatting

21. **Bold labels and decorative lists.** No bold inside a post. No list where each
    item starts with a bold label and a colon; write it as sentences.
22. **Emoji as decoration.** No ✅ 🚀 💡 👉 ✨ bullets or sign-offs unless the person's
    voice notes show them.
23. **Markdown and curly quotes.** LinkedIn shows asterisks and pound signs literally.
    Use straight quotes.

### E. Leftovers and performance

24. **Chatbot residue.** "Great question", "I hope this helps", "Let me know",
    "Certainly". Never in a post.
25. **Announced sincerity.** "Let me be honest", "I'll be real", "Confession:",
    "Unpopular opinion:" before a popular one, "Can I be vulnerable for a second".
    If the material has an uncomfortable fact, state it flat with its date and no
    frame: "We lost our biggest customer on 14 February." If it does not, do not
    manufacture one.
26. **Writing about the post instead of the subject.** "In this post I'll…", "Here's
    a breakdown", "Let me walk you through". Start with the subject.

## When not to act

A person can make any of these choices on purpose. Leave a watched phrase alone inside
a quotation, a title or a name. Keep what carries a voice: an unusual specific
detail, mixed feelings stated plainly, a real aside in parentheses, a first-person
choice the writer explains, slang the person really uses. Several tells together are
the signal; one alone rarely is.

What makes a post sound like a person, in the end, is material: a number with its
source, a named thing, a real reaction, an opinion with its reason. Those come from
the item, the story, `about.md` and the person's own posts. Use what is there, write
it plainly, and leave out what is not.
